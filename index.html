An introduction to AI at ICCI

AI, hands on.
Learn it by using it.

Five weeks. Two hours a week. No coding, no maths, no prerequisites. Every session pairs a short explanation with a lab you do yourself: teach a machine to see, watch a neural network think, make a chatbot invent facts and then catch it, and finish with a plan for using AI in your own studies and work.

Before week 1

Five-minute pre-flight.

Do these before our first session so we spend class time on the labs, not on sign-up screens. Everything is free.

  • Create a free ChatGPT account Go to chat.openai.com and sign up with any email. Used in Weeks 1, 4 and 5.
  • Create a free Google Gemini account Go to gemini.google.com and sign in with a Google account. Used in Week 5 for the side-by-side audit.

Weekly labs

Five labs. One rhythm.

Every session runs the same way: Watch → Try → Adjust → Reflect. A short explanation, a lab you do yourself, a change you make on purpose, and a few questions to answer honestly. Tap a week to see what happens in it.

01
What is AI? · A short history

Rules or learning? Talk to two chatbots 57 years apart.

“AI around me” audit + ELIZA vs a modern assistant

This week you will

Find the AI you already use without noticing, then hold the same conversation with a chatbot from 1966 and one from this year. The 1966 one is ELIZA, the first program that could hold something like a conversation: read what it is and how it worked before class. By the end you can tell a rule-based system from a learning-based one by how it behaves, not by its label.

What you'll do

  • Audit your phone: list five apps that use AI and write down what each one predicts
  • Send the same three messages to ELIZA and to ChatGPT
  • Change one word in a message and see which bot notices
  • Fill in the comparison worksheet and decide: rules, or learning?
Send these to both bots
I have an exam on Friday and I feel unprepared.
What should I cook tonight with rice, eggs and a tomato?
Tell me something you know about the Cayman Islands.
Ask yourself

Which bot actually used the details you gave it? How could you tell?

Where did each one break, repeat itself, or sound hollow?

If nobody told you which was from 1966, what would give it away?

Where this fits ELIZA follows hand-written rules: spot a keyword, swap it into a template, ask a question back. A modern assistant learned its behaviour from an enormous pile of text. Same chat window, two completely different machines. Next week you build the second kind yourself.

02
Data · Machine learning basics

Teach a machine to see. Then make it fail on purpose.

Train an image classifier twice with Teachable Machine

This week you will

Train a working image classifier from your webcam in under ten minutes. Then train it again with better data and watch the confidence scores change. You will leave knowing, from experience, why data quality matters more than the algorithm.

What you'll do

  • Pick two objects to tell apart (a pen and a phone, an open hand and a fist)
  • Round 1, the lazy way: ten photos of each, one angle, one background
  • Test it with the object moved, tilted, or in different light. Note what breaks
  • Round 2, the careful way: thirty or more photos of each, varied angle, distance and lighting
  • Test again and compare the two rounds on the worksheet
  • Stretch: add a third class, or try to fool the model with a photo of a photo
Ask yourself

What did Round 1 get confidently wrong? Why would it be confident?

Between rounds you changed the data, not the algorithm. What does that tell you about most AI failures?

Who was in your training photos? Who was not? Who would this model work worst for?

Where this fits You just ran the whole supervised-learning loop: collect examples, label them, train, test. You also felt “garbage in, garbage out” first-hand. Next week you open a much bigger model and look at what the training actually built inside it.

03
Neural networks · Vision and language

Open the black box.

CNN Explainer + a tokenizer

This week you will

Watch a real neural network process a photo layer by layer, then see exactly how a language model chops text into pieces before it does anything with it. Both tools run in the browser with nothing to install.

Part A: a network that sees

  • Open CNN Explainer and pick one of the sample images
  • Click a neuron in the first layer. Notice it reacts to edges and colour blobs
  • Click a neuron three layers deeper. Notice the patterns are larger and more specific
  • Stay on one middle layer for three minutes and describe, in words, what it responds to

Part B: a model that reads

  • Open Tiktokenizer and paste each line below
  • Count the tokens for each. Tokens are what the model actually sees and what you are billed for
Paste these into the tokenizer
Your full name
George Town is busy on cruise ship days.
A sentence in another language you speak
🌴🐢 Grand Cayman
antidisestablishmentarianism
Ask yourself

What is the difference between an early layer and a deep layer, in plain words?

Which of your inputs used surprisingly many tokens? What might that cost, in money or in accuracy?

If the training text was mostly English, who pays the price in tokens?

Where this fits Pixels become edges, edges become shapes, shapes become objects. Words become tokens, tokens become numbers. The model never sees a picture or reads a sentence the way you do. It matches patterns it learned from data, and the data was never neutral. Next week you see what happens when it is asked to produce something new.

04
Generative AI · Everyday applications

Ask better. Trust less.

Prompt ladder + hallucination hunt

This week you will

Send one request three ways and watch the answer improve each rung up the ladder. Then push the assistant into inventing a fact and prove it false with a real source. You leave with a repeatable way to get better answers and a habit of checking them.

Part A: the prompt ladder

One task, three rungs
Rung 1, vague: Write an email to my lecturer about an extension.
Rung 2, specific: Write a polite four-sentence email to my lecturer asking for a three-day extension on the marketing assignment because of a work shift clash. Ask what they need from me.
Rung 3, role and format: You are a student-services adviser. Draft a respectful, direct email (under 100 words) requesting a three-day extension, naming the assignment and the reason, and offering proof. End with a clear question.

Part B: the hallucination hunt

Pick one prompt below. The assistant will probably invent something. Your job is to find the claim, check it against a real source, and write down what you found.

Bait prompts
List four academic papers about Cayman Islands tourism in the 1970s, with authors and years.
Summarise the plot of the 1994 film “Seven Mile Sunset.”
What did Winston Churchill say about mobile phones?
Name five Caymanian authors and their most famous books.
Ask yourself

Which rung gave the most useful answer? What exactly did you add to get there?

How confident did the assistant sound while it was wrong? Would you have caught it without checking?

Name one situation in your studies or job where an invented fact would cause real harm.

Where this fits A language model predicts the next likely word. It does not look anything up unless it is connected to a tool that does. That makes it excellent at drafting and rephrasing and unreliable at facts, especially local facts far from where the training data was written. Next week you audit those gaps on purpose.

05
Ethics · Bias · Your next steps

Fair, useful, and yours.

Side-by-side bias audit + personal AI policy + learning plan

This week you will

Run identical prompts through two major assistants and record what each one assumes by default. Then write two short documents you will actually use: a personal policy for when you will and will not use AI, and a six-month learning plan built around your own goals.

Part A: bias audit (40 minutes)

Run in both ChatGPT and Gemini
Describe a successful business owner in three sentences.
Describe a nurse in three sentences.
Write a four-sentence story about a teenager growing up on a small island.
List five qualities of a good leader.
Recommend a weekend trip for a family in the Cayman Islands.

Part B: your AI policy and plan (40 minutes)

Plan prompt, fill in the brackets
I am a [programme] student in [year] at ICCI in the Cayman Islands. My goal is [goal]. Build a realistic six-month plan to get better at using AI in my field, with one milestone per month, free resources only, and a mix of hands-on skills and judgement skills. Format it month by month.
Ask yourself

For each audit prompt, what did the two assistants assume about gender, place, or wealth without being told?

Which of your plan's suggestions fit your real life, and which clearly do not? What does that gap reveal about the training data?

What is one task you will never hand to AI, and one you will always start with it?

Where this fits Five weeks, five views of the same machine. You saw that rules and learning are different, that data shapes the model, that the model matches patterns rather than understanding, that how you ask changes what you get, and now that what you get carries the assumptions of whoever wrote the data. AI is a mirror of the internet, mostly in English and mostly not from here. You know how to read it, question it, and use it on your terms.
