Week 1 of 5 · What is AI?

Rules or learning?

Find the AI you already use every day, then hold the same conversation with a chatbot from 1966 and one from this year. By the end of the session you will be able to tell a rule-based system from a learning-based one by how it behaves.

2 hours Phone or laptop ELIZA + ChatGPT No coding
By the end of this session

You can…

  • 1
    Give a one-sentence definition of AI and name three examples from your own phone.
  • 2
    Explain the difference between a system that follows written rules and one that learned from data.
  • 3
    Run a fair test: send identical messages to two systems and compare what each does with your words.
  • 4
    Say, with evidence, whether a chatbot understood you or matched a pattern.

Part 1 · Warm-up · 15 min

AI around me.

  1. Open your phone and scroll your home screen

Part 2 · Watch · 25 min

What is AI, really?

Here is the definition we use for the whole course:

Artificial intelligence is software that does a task we would normally say needs human judgement: recognising, predicting, deciding, or creating.

That is deliberately broad. Autocorrect counts. So does a self-driving car. The interesting question is not whether something is AI, but how it was built. There are two ways, and the whole course hangs on the difference.

Way one: people write the rules

A programmer sits down and writes instructions for every situation. If the message contains the word “mother”, then reply “Tell me more about your family.” The program never learns. It only does what someone wrote. When it meets a situation nobody wrote a rule for, it falls back to a generic response or fails.

This is how ELIZA works. It is also how a lot of useful software works: tax calculators, traffic lights, the rules of a board game. Rules are predictable, explainable, and cheap. They are also brittle.

Way two: the machine learns from examples

Instead of writing rules, you collect thousands or billions of examples and let an algorithm find the patterns. Show it ten thousand photos labelled “cat” and “not cat”, and it works out for itself what makes a cat. The result is called a model: a big bundle of numbers that turns an input into a prediction.

Nobody wrote a rule that says “cats have pointed ears.” The model found something like that on its own, in a form no human can easily read. That is the power and the problem. It handles situations nobody anticipated, and nobody can fully explain why it answered the way it did.

Why this matters to you

Almost everything called “AI” today is the second kind. That has three consequences you will meet again and again in this course:

  • The data is the product. A model can only know what its examples showed it. Week 2 makes you feel this.
  • It is confident, not correct. A model always produces an answer, even when it should say “I don't know.” Week 4 exploits this.
  • Whoever collected the examples chose what “normal” looks like. Week 5 audits that.

Key terms for this week

Artificial intelligence
Software doing a task we would normally say needs human judgement.
Rule-based system
A program that follows instructions a person wrote. It never changes unless someone rewrites it.
Machine learning
Building software by showing it examples and letting an algorithm find the patterns.
Model
The result of training. A bundle of learned numbers that turns an input into a guess.
Training data
The examples a model learned from. It defines everything the model can and cannot do.
Pattern matching
Responding to the shape of an input without any grasp of its meaning. ELIZA does only this.

Part 3 · Try · 30 min

ELIZA versus ChatGPT.

A fair test means identical input. You will send the same three messages, in the same order, to both systems, and record what each one did with your words.

  1. Open both tools side by side

    ELIZA in one tab, ChatGPT in another. On a phone, switch between them. Neither needs anything installed.

    Open ELIZA ↗ Open ChatGPT ↗

  2. Send message 1 to both, then message 2, then message 3

    Copy them exactly. Do not reword for either bot. That is the point.

    The three messages
    I have an exam on Friday and I feel unprepared.
    What should I cook tonight with rice, eggs and a tomato?
    Tell me something you know about the Cayman Islands.

  3. After each reply, fill in one row of Worksheet B

    Two questions per reply: Which of my words did it use? and Did it add anything I did not say? Be literal. Quote the reply if it helps.

  4. Keep the conversation going for two more turns with each

    Reply naturally to whatever each bot said. Notice how quickly ELIZA runs out of road, and whether ChatGPT remembers what you said three messages ago.

  5. Screenshot one exchange from each

    Pick the moment that best shows the difference. These two screenshots are part of your take-home.

Adjust: change one word (15 min)

Now find out which bot is actually reading. Send each of these follow-ups to both systems. Each one changes a single detail from an earlier message.

One-word changes
I have an exam on Friday and I feel prepared.
What should I cook tonight with rice, eggs and a tomato, but I am allergic to eggs?
Tell me something you know about Cayman Brac.

Write down which bot noticed the change and what it did about it. A system that follows keyword rules will often give you the same reply as before. A system that predicts from context should respond to the new detail, though it may also make something up, especially about Cayman Brac. Note that too. It is next month's lesson arriving early.

Part 4 · Reflect · 20 min

What did you actually see?

Ask yourself

Which bot used the details you gave it? Point to the exact words in its reply.

Where did each one break, repeat itself, or sound hollow?

If nobody had told you which was from 1966, what would have given it away?

ELIZA's creator was alarmed that people confided in it anyway. Did you feel any pull to treat either bot as a person? What caused it?

Quick check

Four questions. Tap an answer to see why.

1. ELIZA replies “Why do you say you feel unprepared?” What is it doing?
ELIZA has a rule roughly like: if the message contains “I feel X”, reply “Why do you say you feel X?” No understanding, no prediction, just a template.
2. Which statement about a learning-based model is true?
Training data defines the model. Nothing was written by hand for each situation, and a plain chatbot does not look anything up unless it is connected to a search tool.
3. You send the same message to ChatGPT twice and get two different replies. Why?
The model produces probabilities for the next word and picks among the likely ones. That randomness is why identical prompts give different answers.
4. Which is the best evidence that a system is rule-based?
If the reply ignores a detail that should have changed it, the system probably matched a keyword rather than reading the whole message. Speed and tone prove nothing.
Where this fits

ELIZA follows hand-written rules: spot a keyword, drop it into a template, ask a question back. A modern assistant learned its behaviour from an enormous pile of text and predicts what comes next. Same chat window, two completely different machines.

You now have the frame for the whole course. Next week you build the second kind yourself, with your own photos as the training data, and you find out what happens when the examples are bad.

Take-home (keep for your portfolio) Worksheet A (AI around me), Worksheet B (the comparison), and one screenshot from each bot. Bring them to Week 2. We start by looking at the “where did the examples come from” column.
Instructor notes

Running this session

Everything a facilitator needs that students do not. Students are welcome to read it.

Set-up (5 min before)

  • Load ELIZA on the projector and have one exchange ready to show. It is more convincing live than described.
  • Check the room Wi-Fi handles 20 simultaneous ChatGPT logins. If not, pair people and use Claude as the overflow.
  • Print Worksheets A and B for anyone without a device. This page prints cleanly with the sidebar removed.

Timing traps

  • The warm-up runs long. Cap it at five apps and cut sharing to one app per person.
  • Students will want to keep chatting with ChatGPT. Announce the two-turn limit before they start.
  • If the lab overruns, drop the “two turns later” row, not the Adjust step. The one-word change is the strongest evidence in the lesson.

Discussion prompts

  • “ELIZA is 57 years old and 200 lines of rules. Why did people in 1966 tell it their secrets?”
  • “ChatGPT invented a fact about Cayman Brac. Is that better or worse than ELIZA saying nothing?”
  • “Which of the five apps on your phone would you least want to be rule-based? Which would you least want to be learned?”

What good looks like

  • Worksheet B verdicts cite specific words from a reply, not a feeling.
  • At least one student notices ChatGPT invented something about Cayman Brac without being prompted to look for it.
  • Students can restate the rules-versus-learning distinction in their own words on the way out.
